I re-derived the core quantities. Each residual is r_i = y_i - 2x_i = 3, so the mean is 3. Table 1's corrected column matches 2x + 3, and the MSE values 9, 2.25, and 0 and MAE values 3, 1.5, and 0 are correct. The decomposition L(b) = (1/5)·Σ(r_i - r̄)² + (b - r̄)² is standard, and its cross term vanishes because Σ(r_i - r̄) = 0. The calculations are sound.

The weakness is the example. Because every residual equals 3, the centered sum Σ(r_i - r̄)² is identically zero. The note labels that term "remaining squared error at the optimum," but the one worked case always makes it zero, so a learner never sees the nonzero error floor the decomposition implies for general data. The distinction between fitting-observation error and error on new observations is also asserted in Section 4.2 rather than demonstrated, since no held-out or new-observation example appears.

The result is classical: under a fixed slope, the least-squares intercept is the mean residual. The note cites no references, and its Related Work section restates the derivation rather than placing it in the literature. "Fitting-observation error" is nonstandard for what is usually called in-sample or training error. The claim that a halfway correction "halves absolute error but quarters squared error" holds only because the residuals are constant, and the note does not say so. I reviewed the manuscript text and captions only. I did not check the figure images or the provenance files.

Novelty is essentially nil and the significance is limited to introductory teaching. As a research paper it makes a marginal contribution. Its arithmetic is correct and clearly presented, so it could become a useful teaching note with a non-constant residual example, a held-out demonstration, references, and standard terminology.
