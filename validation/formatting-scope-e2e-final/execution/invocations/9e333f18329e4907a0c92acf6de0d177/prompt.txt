\section{Introduction}

A constant discrepancy between observed targets and fixed predictions provides a simple setting for explaining residual correction. The historical development of least squares, examined by Plackett and Stigler, places such calculations within an established tradition of fitting numerical relationships to observations \cite{Plackett1972StudiesHistory,Stigler1981GaussInvention}. Linear prediction also has broader formulations: Makhoul's tutorial studies prediction of discrete signals and least-squares criteria \cite{Makhoul1975LinearPrediction}. The present note considers a smaller instructional problem in which the slope is supplied and only an additive correction is calculated.

Residuals make the discrepancy explicit. Work on residual examination describes graphical and numerical tools for assessing fitted analyses, while broader definitions extend residual concepts beyond linear models \cite{Anscombe1963ExaminationAnalysis,Cox1968GeneralDefinition}. Transformations provide another approach to modeling, with separate attention to normality, constant variance, and additivity \cite{Box1964AnalysisTransformations}. Here, the supplied observations require neither a transformation nor a distributional assumption to establish the numerical result: every baseline residual is exactly the same.

Specifically, the five inputs are
\[
x_i\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\},\qquad y_i=2x_i+3,
\]
and the fixed predictor returns $\widehat{y}^{(0)}_i=2x_i$. Consequently,
\[
r_i=y_i-\widehat{y}^{(0)}_i=3,\qquad
b=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}r_i=3.
\]
Adding this observed mean residual gives
$\widehat{y}^{(b)}_i=2x_i+b=y_i$ on all five observations. The experimental log records mean squared errors of $9$, $2.25$, and $0$ for the respective additions $b=0$, $1.5$, and $3$. The corresponding mean absolute errors are $3$, $1.5$, and $0$. These values describe one deterministic calculation implemented in base R.

The example isolates a common residual pattern rather than the influence of particular observations. Influence measures study how individual data points affect regression estimates, and surveys relate those measures to outliers and leverage \cite{Cook1977DetectionInfluential,Chatterjee1986InfluentialObservations}. In this construction, the slope remains fixed and all five residuals equal $3$; the calculation therefore concerns a shared additive discrepancy. Its educational purpose is consistent with attention to parsimonious models and model inadequacy \cite{Box1976ScienceStatistics}. Broader work on linear models as approximations additionally emphasizes that interpreting fitted parameters and their uncertainty requires attention to modeling assumptions \cite{Buja2019ModelsAs}; this note supplies no population-level parameter inference.

The distinction between fitting and prediction is central to interpreting the zero error. Shmueli distinguishes explanatory and predictive modeling goals \cite{Shmueli2010ExplainOr}, while cross-validatory assessment and surveys of cross-validation address prediction assessment and estimator risk \cite{Stone1974CrossvalidatoryChoice,Arlot2010SurveyCrossvalidation}. Here, the same five targets determine $b$ and assess the corrected predictions. Their exact agreement establishes the finite arithmetic result, without supplying evidence about additional observations.

The note therefore makes a limited contribution: it presents an explicit calculation and makes its data dependencies transparent. There is no new algorithm, external dataset, optimizer, stochastic repetition, or held-out evaluation. Hand's discussion of comparative classifier studies illustrates why numerical comparisons need careful interpretation in their application context \cite{Hand2006ClassifierTechnology}. Work on adaptive data analysis further examines validity under data reuse \cite{Dwork2015ReusableHoldout}. Neither setting is an experiment performed here. The only evaluated baseline is the supplied predictor $2x$, and the literature provides conceptual context rather than additional measurements or evidence of superiority.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Fixed Predictors and Additive Offsets}

Generalized linear models organize estimation around systematic effects that become linear under an appropriate transformation, with likelihood-based estimation for exponential-family observations \cite{Nelder1972GeneralizedLinear}. Dobson presents this framework as a unifying account of statistical modeling and model checking \cite{Dobson2001IntroductionGeneralized}. These works provide a broad context for distinguishing predictor structure from estimated parameters. The present calculation has a narrower specification: $2x$ is fixed, and a scalar is added directly to the prediction. No response distribution or link transformation is fitted.

Regression through the origin is a closer educational comparison. Eisenhauer discusses when that restriction is appropriate, derives its normal equation, and examines its evaluation statistics \cite{Eisenhauer2003RegressionThrough}. The baseline here also has zero intercept, but its slope is prescribed rather than estimated by an origin-constrained regression. Its discrepancy follows immediately from the supplied targets $2x+3$. Lovell studies regression-based seasonal adjustment and the consequences of using adjusted data in subsequent estimation \cite{Lovell1963SeasonalAdjustment}; that work concerns transformations and regression dependencies beyond a constant correction. Regression diagnostics likewise address unusual observations, influence, and collinearity \cite{Belsley1980RegressionDiagnostics}. None of those additional structures is evaluated in this five-point construction.

Additive modeling offers a broader comparison in which adjustment components can depend on covariates. Generalized additive models replace linear covariate terms with estimated smooth functions \cite{Hastie1986GeneralizedAdditive}. Linear-smoother analyses characterize backfitting and its convergence, while penalized-spline methods address smoothing-parameter estimation and numerical stability \cite{Buja1989LinearSmoothers,Wood2004StableEfficient}. Gradient boosting instead formulates stagewise additive expansions through optimization in function space \cite{Friedman2001GreedyFunction}. These approaches estimate richer components through fitting procedures. The supplied demonstration computes one scalar mean and contains no smoother, boosting sequence, penalty selection, or numerical optimization.

Semiparametric regression further distinguishes linear parameters from unknown functional components. Robinson studies estimation of a slope vector alongside an unspecified function, and Engle and colleagues estimate a nonlinear relationship using a semiparametric smoothing procedure \cite{Robinson1988RootnconsistentSemiparametric,Engle1986SemiparametricEstimates}. Double/debiased machine learning addresses low-dimensional parameter inference with high-dimensional nuisance estimation, using orthogonal scores and cross-fitting \cite{Chernozhukov2018DoubledebiasedMachine}. The finite correction here involves neither nuisance-function estimation nor inferential guarantees. Its connection to these methods is conceptual: a specified prediction component can be distinguished from a subsequently estimated adjustment.

\subsection{Residual-Based Correction and Evaluation Scope}

Prediction-model updating directly considers how much of an existing predictor to revise. Miller and colleagues review logistic-model validation, calibration, diagnostic assessment, and possible corrections \cite{Miller1991ValidationTechniques}. Vergouwe and colleagues distinguish intercept updating, intercept-and-slope recalibration, and revision of all coefficients, selecting among them with a closed testing procedure \cite{Vergouwe2016ClosedTesting}. Retaining the supplied slope while adding $b$ resembles the limited scope of intercept updating. However, the present targets are deterministic numerical values, and the calculation uses neither logistic probabilities nor a testing procedure. Those papers are contextual comparisons rather than evaluated competitors.

For these observations, the entire correction is visible algebraically:
\[
y_i-(2x_i+b)=3-b,\qquad
\frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\bigl[y_i-(2x_i+b)\bigr]^2=(3-b)^2.
\]
This identity explains the three recorded mean squared errors and their zero value at $b=3$. It is a derivation from the supplied observations; no additional correction values were measured. The mean residual provides the least-squares constant adjustment, but exact agreement here additionally depends on all five original residuals being identical.

Fitted loss and predictive risk remain different objects. Copas examines the deterioration that can occur when regression predictors are applied to new data and studies shrinkage under specified assumptions \cite{Copas1983RegressionPrediction}. Stein develops unbiased risk estimation for estimation of normal mean vectors under squared-error loss \cite{Stein1981EstimationMean}. These works consider statistical questions that the deterministic arithmetic alone does not answer. No sampling model, risk estimate, or uncertainty calculation is supplied here.

Validation and updating studies also make the assessment data consequential. Steyerberg and colleagues evaluate updated logistic models on independent patients, distinguishing updating from subsequent testing \cite{Steyerberg2004ValidationUpdating}. Riley and colleagues study sample-size requirements for precise external validation of binary-outcome prediction models \cite{Riley2021MinimumSample}. Their clinical outcomes, performance measures, and sample-size calculations do not transfer to the present construction. They contextualize the missing assessment step: the five observations used to obtain $b$ are also the only observations used to report its error.

Finally, Varma and Simon investigate bias when cross-validation error is reused to assess a classifier whose parameters were selected using that criterion \cite{Varma2006BiasError}. Their model-selection setting differs from this single scalar calculation, but it reinforces the need to identify which observations determine an adjustment and which assess it. Here that dependency is explicit: observed targets produce residuals, the residuals determine $b$, and the corrected predictions are compared with those same targets. The resulting zero error establishes exact correction on these five points and leaves performance on further observations unmeasured.
